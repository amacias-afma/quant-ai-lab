## C. Power — the control that broke our headline

- **[V] Button, K. S., Ioannidis, J. P. A., Mokrysz, C., Nosek, B. A., Flint, J.,

  *(An erratum exists; check it before quoting figures.)*
  *Use:* the direct precedent for §3.2. Their argument transfers unchanged: an underpowered
  null is not evidence of absence.
  **The comparison is worth stating explicitly:** they estimate median power in the
  neurosciences at **8–31%**. Our design had **9.5%** power against its own observed effect —

  pre-registered and believed itself careful.

- **Power practice in financial forecasting — a SEARCH NULL, written as one.**
  Across the five ML-VaR papers read in full (`survey-ml-var.md`) and the targeted searches run
  for this bibliography, **no paper reporting a minimum detectable effect or a power calculation

  is pressing — P2 on 250 rolling forecasts per index, our own study on ~750 observations per

  **This is not evidence that no such paper exists.** No systematic search was run, no frame was
  specified, and by the standard §3.2 applies to this study's own nulls, "we did not find it"

  A reviewer who knows of one improves the paper; that is the correct outcome and we should
  invite it explicitly.

## H. Permutation and placebo controls — the control's lineage

**Status: MET.** The shuffled-prior control of §3.4 is **not a new technique**. It is a
restricted randomization test, a method with a seventy-year lineage and at least four
independent traditions. Saying so plainly is the point of this section: a control with
ancestry is more credible than one presented as an invention, and claiming novelty for an
adaptation is the exact overreach this paper exists to criticise.

### H.1 The permutation test proper

- **[V] Fisher, R. A. (1935).** *The Design of Experiments*. Edinburgh: Oliver & Boyd.
  **Chapter III, "A Historical Experiment on Growth Rate," §21 "Test of a Wider Hypothesis."**
  *Use:* the origin. Fisher introduced randomization tests to replace Student's *t* where
  normality fails, and to make randomization "the physical basis of the validity of statistical
  tests." The logic our control uses is his: construct the null by rearranging the data, not by
  assuming a distribution for it.
  > **Correction made during verification.** This entry previously read "ch. 21", copied from
  > secondary sources that describe the randomization test as appearing in "Chapter 21."
  > *The Design of Experiments* has roughly ten chapters with continuously numbered sections;
  > the randomization test is **§21 within Chapter III**. The error was inherited from the
  > literature, which is how most citation errors are acquired.

- **[V] Pitman, E. J. G. (1937).** "Significance Tests Which May be Applied to Samples from Any
  Populations." *Supplement to the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society* **4(1), 119–130.**
  *Use:* the immediate generalisation; the standard pairing is "Fisher–Pitman." One citation,
  for lineage only — we do not use their machinery.

### H.2 Restricted randomization — the closest ancestor, and the one that names our mistake

- **[V] Ojala, M. & Garriga, G. C. (2010).** "Permutation Tests for Studying Classifier
  Performance." *Journal of Machine Learning Research* 11, 1833–1863.
  **This is the paper §3.4 should have been read before running.** Ojala and Garriga study two
  permutation tests: permuting labels (does the classifier find real structure?) and permuting
  **features within classes** — a *restricted* randomization that destroys the dependency under
  test while preserving other properties of the data. Our shuffled prior is the second kind:
  the marginal distribution, mean and standard deviation are preserved; only the temporal
  correspondence with tomorrow's return is destroyed.

  More pointedly, they state the design principle we arrived at empirically and expensively:

  > "each randomization method entails a certain null distribution, that is, **which properties
  > of the original data are preserved in the randomization test**, directly affecting the
  > distribution of the error"

  Our §3.4 "trap" — pooling all controls gave an apparent 1.32× vs 2.36× gap, produced entirely
  by a control shrinking toward an **off-scale** target — is a rediscovery of exactly this. The
  off-scale control fights the data rather than shrinking within it, so it induces a different
  null. We found this by tripping over it. **It has a name (restricted randomization) and a
  citation, and the paper must say so rather than presenting the scale-matching requirement as
  an insight of ours.**

### H.3 Randomization as a sanity check in machine learning

- **[V] Zhang, C., Bengio, S., Hardt, M., Recht, B. & Vinyals, O. (2017).** "Understanding Deep
  Learning Requires Rethinking Generalization." *ICLR 2017* (arXiv:1611.03530); revised as
  *CACM* 64(3), 2021. *Use:* the canonical random-label experiment — networks fit random labels
  perfectly, so capacity-based explanations of generalisation cannot be right. Same move as
  ours: run the procedure against a target with no information and see what survives.

- **[V] Adebayo, J., Gilmer, J., Muelly, M., Goodfellow, I., Hardt, M. & Kim, B. (2018).**
  "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps." *NeurIPS 2018.*
  *Use:* **the closest match in spirit.** Their model-parameter and data randomization tests ask
  whether a saliency method's output changes when the model or the labels are randomized; methods
  whose output is invariant are not explaining the model, whatever they look like. Our question
  is the same one asked of a different quantity: *does the reported benefit change when the
  anchor's information is destroyed?* It does not, so the benefit was not coming from the anchor.
  We should cite this as the template and claim only the transfer.

### H.4 Placebo and falsification tests in econometrics

- **[V] Bertrand, M., Duflo, E. & Mullainathan, S. (2004).** "How Much Should We Trust
  Differences-in-Differences Estimates?" *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 119(1), 249–275.
  *Use:* the strongest precedent for the *shape* of our result. They generate **placebo laws** at
  random and find conventional DD standard errors reject at 5% for **up to 45%** of them. A
  method producing significance against a target known to carry no signal is broken, and the
  demonstration is the placebo run — not a better test statistic. Our shuffled prior is the same
  argument applied to a variance claim rather than a treatment effect.

- **[V] Abadie, A., Diamond, A. & Hainmueller, J. (2010).** "Synthetic Control Methods for
  Comparative Case Studies: Estimating the Effect of California's Tobacco Control Program."
  *Journal of the American Statistical Association* **105(490), 493–505.**
  *Use:* one line, for breadth of the placebo tradition — their inference is placebo-based,
  reassigning the treatment to untreated units to build a null distribution.

### H.7 What is inherited and what, narrowly, is not

Stated so a referee does not have to extract it:

| element | status |
|---|---|
| permutation/randomization test | **inherited** — Fisher (1935), Pitman (1937) |
| restricted randomization; preserving chosen properties | **inherited** — Ojala & Garriga (2010) |
| randomize the input a claim depends on, see if the claim survives | **inherited** — Adebayo et al. (2018); Zhang et al. (2017) |
| placebo target as the demonstration of a broken method | **inherited** — Bertrand et al. (2004) |
| the shrinkage mechanism itself | **inherited** — Stein (1956); James & Stein (1961); Hoerl & Kennard (1970), §G |
| applying the control to a **variance/stability** claim rather than an accuracy claim | **no instance located** |

The last row is a **search null, not a proven gap**, and must be written that way. By the
standard §3.2 applies to this study's own nulls, "we did not find it" is not "it does not
exist." The defensible sentence is: *this control is standard in four literatures; we found no
instance of it applied to a stability claim, and offer ours as a template rather than as a
method.*

## I. Selection on a small validation block — the lineage of §3.2

**Status: MET.** Our §3.2 reports that validation-selected anchor weights were worse than
`w = 0` in 6 of 16 comparisons, and that the design had 9.5% power to say anything about it.
That result has a literature, it is twenty years old, and it explains our finding better than
we did.

- **[V] Cawley, G. C. & Talbot, N. L. C. (2010).** "On Over-fitting in Model Selection and
  Subsequent Selection Bias in Performance Evaluation." *Journal of Machine Learning Research*
  **11, 2079–2107.**
  **This is the paper §3.2 should have cited from the start.** Their thesis is that the model
  selection criterion is itself an estimator with a variance, and that optimising it over a
  finite sample **over-fits the criterion** exactly as training over-fits the data:

  > "a low **variance** is at least as important [as unbiasedness], as a non-negligible variance
  > introduces the potential for **over-fitting in model selection** as well as in training the
  > model"

  Three consequences bear directly on this paper:

  1. **The magnitude warning.** *"the effects of this form of over-fitting are often of
     comparable magnitude to differences in performance between learning algorithms, and thus
     cannot be ignored in empirical evaluation."* Our observed anchoring edges were 1–8% of the
     loss level (§3.2). Cawley and Talbot say selection over-fitting operates at that same scale.
     **Our comparison may have been conducted entirely inside the noise floor of its own
     selection step**, which is a stronger statement than "underpowered" and should be said.
  2. **The mechanism for our 37.5%.** They deliberately use *"a split-sample based model
     selection strategy with a relatively high variance, due to the limited size of the
     validation set"* — our design. Their Figure 8 shows that enlarging the validation sample
     tightens the selected hyper-parameters around the optimum. They also note the criterion
     surface is often *"a broad valley"*, so a badly-selected value can still generalise well.
     **That is a better explanation of our result than the one §3.2 gives**: not that selection
     carries no signal, but that the surface is flat and the block is short.
  3. **A remedy we cannot fully adopt, and must say so.** *"evaluation of machine learning
     algorithms should always involve multiple partitions of the data to form
     training/validation and test sets, as the sampling of data for a single partition might
     arbitrarily favour one classifier over another."* We used a **single chronological
     partition**, because the data are time series and shuffling would leak. Their remedy is
     therefore only partly available to us — the honest version is multiple *chronological*
     origins, which we did not run. **This is a limitation, not a defence.**

- **[V] Varma, S. & Simon, R. (2006).** "Bias in error estimation when using cross-validation
  for model selection." *BMC Bioinformatics* **7, 91.**
  *Use:* the same result reached independently in bioinformatics four years earlier — an error
  estimate computed on data already used for tuning is *substantially biased*, and the remedy is
  nested cross-validation. Relevant to §4: our test block informed design decisions between
  passes, which is this bias with the loop opened up across months instead of folds.

- **[V] Bengio, Y. & Grandvalet, Y. (2004).** "No Unbiased Estimator of the Variance of K-Fold
  Cross-Validation." *Journal of Machine Learning Research* **5, 1089–1105.**
  *Use:* an impossibility result worth one sentence. There is **no universal unbiased estimator
  of the variance of k-fold CV**, so the uncertainty attached to a selection criterion cannot in
  general be estimated cleanly. Supports our decision to report inter-seed IQR and bootstrap
  intervals over comparisons rather than a single standard error.

- **[V] Bergstra, J. & Bengio, Y. (2012).** "Random Search for Hyper-Parameter Optimization."
  *Journal of Machine Learning Research* **13, 281–305.**
  *Use:* one line on search design. Our anchor weight was chosen by grid; they show random
  search is more efficient over the same budget. Not a defect in our study — the grid was
  one-dimensional and pre-registered — but the right citation if a referee asks why a grid.

**What this section changes.** §3.2 currently frames the selection result as *undetermined for
want of power*. That is true and insufficient. The fuller statement is that **a selection step
optimising a high-variance criterion over a short validation block is a known failure mode with
a known magnitude**, and our design had it. We report the power calculation because we ran it;
we should report Cawley and Talbot because they predicted the result before we obtained it.

**None came from carelessness about the citation itself.** Four were inherited from other

**A citation copied from a citation is not a verified citation**, and five of twenty is a rate
we would not have guessed.

One coda worth recording without irony. The Lin et al. article — the one whose date we got
wrong — **carries its own published correction** (29 February 2024) stating that *"the citation
information to Table 1 was incorrect."* Its authors explicitly follow a method of their own
devising called *precise citation*. If a paper about citation accuracy, written by authors who
published a paper about citation accuracy, needs a citation correction, then five in twenty is
not a story about anyone being careless. **It is a base rate, and the only defence against a
base rate is a procedure.**

The Madani case is the instructive one: not only were the authors wrong, the **claim** was
backwards. It had been recorded as a possible instance of stability used as a quality signal;
they in fact *prove bounds* relating disagreement to generalization error. Had it gone into the
paper unchecked it would have been a fabricated ally.
